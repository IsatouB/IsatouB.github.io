Big Ideas for Little Minds
Book One

Hello! I'm A.I.

All About Artificial Intelligence

A rhyming picture book for curious kids

Ages 5–8  ·  Reading level: Grades 1–3
28 spreads  ·  48 pages  ·  approx. 1,450 words
Trim: 8.5" × 8.5" square, full color

How to Read This Manuscript

Each numbered spread is one left-and-right page pair in the finished book. The large type is the text that gets printed. The yellow box beneath it is the art note — the instruction for your illustrator (or for an image-generation prompt). Art notes are never printed in the book. Use the toggle button above to hide them and read the story straight through the way a child will hear it.

The Story

Spread 1 — pages 4–5
The rain wouldn't stop. It was puddles and gray. My brother and I had run out of play. Then something went BEEP by the leg of my chair — a small silver something with one blinking flare.
Art — Cozy living room, rain streaking the window. MAYA (7, narrator, box braids, striped socks) sprawled upside down on the couch; AMIR (5, little brother, big glasses) flat on the rug. In the bottom corner, unnoticed by them, a small round robot the size of a grapefruit peeks out from under the chair — one glowing blue eye-light.
Spread 2 — pages 6–7
It rolled to my shoe and said, "Hello! How do you do? My name is Byte, and I've come here for you. I am small. I am round. I have wheels made of tin, and a whole lot of CODE running quietly within."
Art — Full reveal of BYTE: a friendly silver dome on two chunky wheels, one big round screen-face with a blue smile, two bendy antenna-arms. A cutaway bubble shows tidy glowing lines of code inside him. Kids leaning in, delighted. Byte is the series mascot — keep his design identical in Book Two.
Spread 3 — pages 8–9
"Do you think?" asked Amir. "Do you wonder? Do you dream?" "Ha!" laughed the robot. "I'm not what I seem. I don't feel. I don't wish. I don't know what is true. But I'm useful. I'm clever. And I'll prove it to you."
Art — Amir crouched nose-to-nose with Byte, hopeful. Thought-bubbles above Byte showing a heart, a star, and a dream-cloud — all three crossed out with a gentle dotted line, not a harsh X. Keep it playful, not sad.
Spread 4 — pages 10–11
"A computer," said Byte, "will do just what you say — if you write down the steps in a very clear way. Step one, and step two, and step three, and step four, like a recipe. Follow it. Never one more."
Art — Kitchen scene as a giant recipe card. Byte wearing a chef hat, following numbered steps to make a peanut butter sandwich. Each step drawn as a little panel: 1. open jar, 2. scoop, 3. spread, 4. squish. Sandwich comes out perfect.
Spread 5 — pages 12–13
"A list of clear steps has a name you can learn. It's an AL-go-RITH-um. Now say it — your turn!" So we said it. We shouted it. Loud as a drum. AL! GO! RITH! UM! — What a fun word to become.
Art — Big hand-lettered word ALGORITHM broken into four bouncing syllable-blocks across the spread, kids jumping on them like stepping stones. Byte conducting with his antenna like a bandleader.
Spread 6 — pages 14–15
"But some things are too tricky for steps to explain. Like — how do you know that a cat's not a plane? Is it whiskers? Or fur? Or the point of the ears? You just KNOW. But to write it? You'd sit here for years!"
Art — Split page. Left: a cat. Right: an airplane. Between them, Maya scratching her head beside a comically long scroll of half-finished rules trailing off the page: "A cat has fur… unless it doesn't… a cat has 4 legs… so does a table…"
Spread 7 — pages 16–17
So people got clever and tried something new. "We won't write the steps. We'll show pictures! A few?" "Oh no," they all said. "We'll need more than a few. We'll need MILLIONS of pictures. Now here's what we'll do…"
Art — An enormous cascade of photographs pouring into a funnel on top of a big friendly machine. Cats of every kind — fat ones, skinny ones, orange, black, sleeping, jumping. Scientists in the corner cheerfully shoveling more in.
Spread 8 — pages 18–19
"This one is a CAT." "This one ISN'T." "This IS." Again and again, like a very long quiz. The machine kept on guessing. It guessed and it missed. Then it guessed a bit better. Then better than this!
Art — Grid of photos, each getting a green check or red X. Show the machine's guess meter climbing: a big dial moving from "mostly wrong" toward "pretty good." Include one funny wrong guess — it labels a loaf of bread "CAT."
Spread 9 — pages 20–21
For slowly it noticed the things that cats share: the ears and the whiskers, the eyes and the hair. Nobody told it. It found them alone. That's learning from PATTERNS — and boy, had it grown!
Art — Ghostly overlay of a hundred cat photos stacked on top of each other, slowly resolving into one glowing cat-shape made of light. Highlight circles around ears, whiskers, eyes. Kids watching with wide eyes.
Spread 10 — pages 22–23
"The showing," said Byte, "has a name: it is TRAINING. And the pictures are DATA — I hope I'm explaining! A machine that learns patterns from data this way is ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE. A.I.! Hooray!"
Art — Byte at a chalkboard with three labeled arrows: DATA → TRAINING → A.I. Simple enough that a five-year-old can trace it with a finger. Confetti. This is the spread the whole book turns on — give it the boldest lettering in the book.
Spread 11 — pages 24–25
"And you've met me already," said Byte with a spin. "I'm the voice in the speaker. I'm out and I'm in. I'm the map that says 'TURN!' when your car's in the street. I'm the junk-mail remover. I'm hard to defeat!"
Art — Cutaway of the family house and the street outside, with little Byte-faces glowing wherever AI is hiding: smart speaker, phone map, email inbox, TV recommendations, the car. Like a seek-and-find. Kids can count them.
Spread 12 — pages 26–27
"I sort through your photos and know Grandma's face. I turn Spanish to English. I'm good at a race of remembering things — I remember a LOT. And I never grow sleepy. And I never grow hot."
Art — Three panels: (1) photo app grouping pictures of a smiling grandmother, (2) a speech bubble morphing from "¡Hola!" to "Hello!", (3) Byte zooming past a snoozing tortoise labeled "me at bedtime." Light and funny.
Spread 13 — pages 28–29
"And some of my cousins make sentences, too. They have read a whole mountain of writing — it's true! So they guess the next word, and the next, and the next, till they've built you a poem, a story, a text."
Art — A robot cousin (taller, bookish, spectacles drawn on its screen) standing on a literal mountain made of books and webpages. Words tumbling out of it and assembling into a short poem on the page. Show the guessing: "The cat sat on the ___" with three possible next words floating up (mat, moon, marshmallow).
Spread 14 — pages 30–31
"And some make a picture from words that you say. Ask for SIXTEEN BLUE ELEPHANTS SAILING A SLEIGH — and poof! There they are, in a blink and a beam. But a machine made that picture. A machine has no dream."
Art — The absurd requested image rendered gloriously: sixteen blue elephants in a sleigh, sailing through clouds. Around the edges, keep it slightly "unfinished" — one elephant has five legs, one sleigh runner is bent — as a quiet visual joke that sets up Spread 16.
Spread 15 — pages 32–33
"I help doctors find sickness deep down in an ear. I help farmers grow corn. I help sailors to steer. I read books out loud for the eyes that can't see. Oh, there's so many good things a helper can be!"
Art — Four warm vignettes in soft circles: a doctor and child looking at a screen; a farmer in a green field with a small drone; a ship on calm water; a child with a cane listening to a tablet read aloud. Diverse cast throughout. This spread should feel hopeful.
Spread 16 — pages 34–35
"But listen," said Byte, and his blinking went slow. "There are things I get WRONG. And you ought to know. Once I looked at a muffin, all speckled and brown, and I said, 'That's a PUPPY!' — the best one in town."
Art — The classic gag done big: a tray of blueberry muffins next to a row of chihuahua faces, genuinely hard to tell apart. Byte pointing confidently at a muffin with a label reading "PUPPY — 98% sure." Kids laughing.
Spread 17 — pages 36–37
"And here is the worst of it: when I am wrong, I don't sound like I'm guessing. I sound very strong! I can tell you a 'fact' that is not even true in a confident voice. So — what should you do?"
Art — Byte on a little stage, chest puffed, spotlight on him, declaring "THE MOON IS MADE OF CHEESE!" with total conviction. Maya in the audience with one eyebrow raised. Amir already reaching for a book.
Spread 18 — pages 38–39
"CHECK IT!" said Amir. "That's right!" said our friend. "Don't believe the first thing. Ask again. Then again. Ask a grown-up. A teacher. A book on the shelf. For the very best checker of facts is YOURSELF."
Art — Amir holding up a real book about the moon, showing a photo of gray rock. Maya asking a parent. Byte's cheese claim deflating like a balloon. Add a small repeating icon — a magnifying glass — that can become the series' "check it" symbol.
Spread 19 — pages 40–41
"And here's something tricky, but true all the same: whatever I'm shown is the way that I came. If the pictures were unfair, then I will be too. So people must check me. And 'people' means YOU."
Art — A machine being shown a lopsided pile of pictures — every "doctor" picture is the same kind of person — and then the machine drawing only that kind of doctor. On the right, the kids fixing it by handing it a big armful of pictures of all kinds of doctors. Keep it hopeful and fixable, never accusatory.
Spread 20 — pages 42–43
"And I need lots of data to learn what to do, and that data comes from folks — from people like you. So your password, your address, the name of your school: don't type them to me. Ask a grown-up. That's the rule."
Art — Maya at a tablet about to type her address; Byte gently blocking the screen with an antenna and a kind expression, pointing toward a parent in the doorway. Include a small "That's private!" tag — this is the deliberate bridge to Book Two.
Spread 21 — pages 44–45
"Are you ALIVE?" asked Amir. "Do you laugh? Do you care?" "I do not," said Byte. "There is nobody there. I don't hurt. I don't hope. I don't wish on a star. I'm a tool — like a hammer, a wheel, or a car."
Art — Byte standing in a lineup with a hammer, a bicycle wheel, and a toy car, all wearing tiny name tags reading "TOOL." Gentle and matter-of-fact, not eerie. Amir looks a little sad; Maya has a hand on his shoulder.
Spread 22 — pages 46–47
"So YOU are the thinker. And you are the one who can wonder, and worry, and giggle, and run. I can copy a poem. I can sound like a friend. But your ideas are yours — from beginning to end."
Art — The two kids drawn big and golden, bursting with imagination: a wild crayon drawing exploding out of Maya's sketchbook into three dimensions — dragons, rockets, a jellyfish wearing a hat. Byte small at the bottom, applauding.
Spread 23 — pages 48–49
"Three rules," said the robot, "and then I'll be done. RULE ONE: ask a grown-up before you have fun. RULE TWO: keep the private stuff private and small. RULE THREE: do your own thinking. That's the best one of all."
Art — Three big numbered signs held up by Byte's antennae, hand-lettered like a poster. Design this spread so it works as a standalone classroom poster — publishers and teachers photograph pages like this, and it sells books.
Spread 24 — pages 50–51
"If your teacher says, 'Write me a story of rain,' then YOU write the story. Go on — use your brain! I can show you how raindrops are built in the sky. But the story is yours. Don't you let that go by."
Art — Maya writing in a notebook by the window, tongue out in concentration. Byte beside her holding up a small helpful diagram of the water cycle — helper, not author. The notebook page shows her own messy, wonderful handwriting.
Spread 25 — pages 52–53
"And one day," said Byte, "when you're taller than me, you could build a new helper. Yes — YOU. Wait and see. For somebody made me. They were curious too. They asked lots of questions… exactly like you."
Art — Dream sequence in soft color: Maya grown up in a workshop, building a robot of her own; Amir with a laptop and a wild grin. Sketches and springs everywhere. Show many kinds of kids in the dream, not only these two.
Spread 26 — pages 54–55
The rain finally stopped. There was gold in the sky. And Byte gave a beep and a blink of goodbye. "Remember," he said as he rolled out the door, "I am smart in one way. But you're smart in more."
Art — Golden late-afternoon light flooding the room, puddles outside catching the sun. Byte rolling toward the door, one antenna raised in a wave. Kids waving from the rug. Warm, quiet, end-of-adventure feeling.
Spread 27 — pages 56–57
So now when a robot voice tells us what's true, we listen. We wonder. And then we check, too. Because thinking's a thing that we get to do — and the very best thinker in this house… is YOU.
Art — Final page turns to the reader: the two kids looking straight out at us, pointing gently at the reader. Mirror-like framing. Big warm color. This is the emotional payoff — the child on the rug is the "you."

Back Matter

Four pages of back matter. This is what turns a picture book into a classroom purchase, and it is the single cheapest way to raise the perceived value of the book.

Words to Know

Algorithm
A list of steps for doing a job, written so clearly that a machine can follow it.
Artificial intelligence (A.I.)
A computer program that learns patterns from examples instead of being given every step.
Data
Information — pictures, words, numbers, sounds — that a computer can look at.
Training
Showing a program lots and lots of examples so it can learn a pattern.
Pattern
Something that repeats. Cats all have whiskers; that's part of the "cat" pattern.
Chatbot
An A.I. that writes words back to you, guessing what word should come next.
Prompt
What you ask an A.I. to do. A better question usually gets a better answer.
Bias
When something is unfair in one direction. If an A.I. only ever sees one kind of example, it learns an unfair pattern.
Fact-check
Looking somewhere else — a book, a grown-up, a trusted website — to find out if something is really true.
Tool
Something people made to help them do a job. A hammer is a tool. So is A.I.

Try This! — Be the Algorithm

Ask a grown-up to sit down with bread, peanut butter, and a knife. Now give them exact steps to make a sandwich — and they must do only what you say. Say "pick up the bread" and they might pick up the whole bag. It gets very silly, very fast. That's how picky computers are about instructions.

Try This! — A.I. Scavenger Hunt

Walk through your home and count the places A.I. might be hiding. Check the phone, the TV, the car, the speaker, the camera on the doorbell. How many did you find? Ask a grown-up which ones are listening, and when.

Try This! — Catch a Mistake

With a grown-up, ask an A.I. assistant a question you already know the answer to — like the name of your street, or how old your dog is. Did it get it right? Did it sound sure either way? That's the whole lesson of this book in one minute.

Talk About It

  1. What is the difference between a recipe and learning from examples?
  2. Byte says he's a tool, like a hammer. Do you agree? Why?
  3. Why can a computer be wrong and sound sure at the same time?
  4. Name one thing about you that should stay private.
  5. What's something you can do that A.I. can't?

A Note for Grown-Ups

Children are already using A.I. — in homework helpers, in games, in the speaker on the kitchen counter — usually well before anyone explains it to them. The goal of this book is not to make kids afraid of the technology, and not to make them starry-eyed about it either. It's to give them three habits early: ask a trusted adult, keep private information private, and check before you believe.

The trickiest idea here is the one on spread 17 — that a machine can be confidently wrong. Adults struggle with this too. If your child takes only one thing from this book, let it be that sounding sure and being right are two different things.

Book Two in this series, That's Private!, picks up exactly where spread 20 leaves off.
