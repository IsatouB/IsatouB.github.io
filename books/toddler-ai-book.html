Big Ideas for Little Minds · First Books
Toddler Edition

Beep! I'm Byte

A First Book About Robots & A.I.
Ages 2–5  ·  Read-aloud / pre-reader
16 spreads  ·  32 pages  ·  187 words
Trim: 8" × 8" square, full color

What changed for ages 2–5

This is not a trimmed version of the 5–8 book. It's a different book that happens to share a mascot. Toddler picture books follow different rules, and I've applied all of them:

  • 187 words instead of 1,450. Four short lines a spread, maximum.
  • One idea per spread. No spread does two jobs.
  • Sound and repetition carry the book. "Beep, beep, beep!" recurs six times — toddlers join in on it by the third read, and that participation is what makes a book get requested again.
  • Concrete nouns only. Cats, muffins, wheels, ice cream. "Algorithm," "data," and "bias" are all gone — they're beyond this age and their absence costs nothing.
  • The concept is reduced to one true thing: a machine learns by looking at lots and lots of things, it gets things wrong, and it is not alive. That's the whole book.
  • It ends on the child, not the robot. Every good toddler book does.

Format note — there are no board books on KDP

Amazon KDP prints paperback and hardcover only. Board books need a specialty printer with a minimum order in the hundreds or thousands of copies, paid up front. So publish this as an 8" × 8" full-color paperback and describe it as a "first book" or "read-aloud picture book" for ages 2–5 — never call it a board book in the listing, because buyers will expect thick pages and leave one-star reviews when they don't get them.

The Story

Spread 1 — pages 2–3
Beep, beep, beep! Look who's here! A little robot. Byte is here!
Art — BYTE rolling in through a doorway, one antenna up in a wave. Big, simple, centered, filling most of the spread. Toddler books need one clear subject per page and lots of empty space around it. Bright, flat color.
Spread 2 — pages 4–5
Byte has wheels. Byte has a light. Byte has arms that bend just right.
Art — Byte alone on a plain bright background with his parts gently labeled by simple pointing lines: wheels, light, arms. Toddlers love naming pages — this one earns pointing fingers and is the page a two-year-old will stop on.
Spread 3 — pages 6–7
Byte can't eat. Byte can't grow. Byte's a MACHINE. Now you know!
Art — Three little panels: Byte in front of a plate of spaghetti, not eating. Byte next to a growth chart, not growing. Then Byte beside a toaster and a washing machine, all three with friendly faces — machines together. Funny, not sad.
Spread 4 — pages 8–9
What can Byte do? Byte can LOOK! Look, Byte, look! What do you see?
Art — Byte's round screen-face lighting up, two big beams of soft light coming from his eye like a friendly flashlight. Expectant, leaning forward. The page should make a child want to turn it.
Spread 5 — pages 10–11
Here's a cat. One little cat. Byte says, "Hmm… a dog? A hat?"
Art — One fluffy orange cat sitting patiently. Byte looking puzzled, with a thought bubble containing a wobbly dog and a floppy hat. Big visual joke — toddlers know it's a cat, and being smarter than the robot is the best feeling in the book.
Spread 6 — pages 12–13
Show Byte more! Two. Three. Four. Big cats. Small cats. MORE! MORE! MORE!
Art — The spread filling up with cats of every shape and color — striped, spotted, fat, skinny, sleeping, jumping. Countable ones in front (two, three, four clearly separated) and a joyful jumble behind. A counting page hidden inside a concept page.
Spread 7 — pages 14–15
Now Byte knows. Byte shouts, "A CAT!" Byte looked and looked. Byte learned, like that!
Art — Byte lit up bright and triumphant, antennae straight up, confetti or sparkles. One cat in front of him with a big label. This is the entire scientific content of the book, and it lands in four words: Byte looked and looked.
Spread 8 — pages 16–17
Byte learns by looking. Look, look, look! Just like you learn from your favorite book.
Art — Split page. Left: Byte studying cats. Right: a toddler in a grown-up's lap with a picture book open, pointing at the page. Deliberate mirror composition — the child sees themselves doing what the robot does. Warm light on the right side.
Spread 9 — pages 18–19
Byte can help. Where's Grandma's face? Beep! Byte found her. There's the place!
Art — A wall of small photographs. Byte's light landing on one — a smiling grandmother — while a toddler claps. Make Grandma warm and specific enough to be somebody's actual grandmother.
Spread 10 — pages 20–21
Byte can help. Which way to go? Byte says, "LEFT!" Now we know!
Art — A simple winding road seen from above with a big friendly arrow pointing left, and a little car on it. Byte riding on the dashboard. Keep the map cartoon-simple — one road, one arrow, one turn.
Spread 11 — pages 22–23
Uh-oh, Byte! That's not a pup. That's a MUFFIN in a cup!
Art — One blueberry muffin in a paper cup, big and central. Byte pointing at it confidently, with a little wobbly puppy in his thought bubble. The toddler equivalent of the whole "AI is confidently wrong" lesson — and it gets the biggest laugh in the book.
Spread 12 — pages 24–25
Byte gets things wrong. Byte isn't always right. So ask your grown-up. They will help you. Beep!
Art — A toddler tugging a parent's sleeve and pointing at the muffin; the parent crouching down to look. Byte beside them, sheepish, one antenna drooping. The behavior being modelled is simply: go ask.
Spread 13 — pages 26–27
Byte can beep. Byte can roll. Byte can find things. Byte is cool!
Art — Byte doing all three at once in a happy motion-blur — beeping, rolling, spotlight on. Cheerful and fast. Sets up the turn on the next spread, so keep this page purely celebratory.
Spread 14 — pages 28–29
But can Byte hug? No, no, no! Can Byte taste your ice cream? NO!
Art — Two funny panels: Byte's stiff metal arms failing to hug a soft teddy bear, and Byte holding an ice cream cone with no mouth to eat it, looking baffled. Ice cream melting down his side. Big laugh, gentle point.
Spread 15 — pages 30–31
Only YOU can. Yes, it's true. Byte is clever… but not like YOU!
Art — The toddler drawn big and golden in the middle of the spread, hugging a bear with one arm and holding an ice cream in the other, mid-giggle. Byte small at their feet, applauding. The child is the biggest thing on the page — that's the whole payoff.
Spread 16 — pages 32–33
Bye-bye, Byte! Beep, beep, beep! Wave good night. Time to sleep.
Art — Dim bedtime blues and warm lamplight. Byte rolling out the bedroom door, antenna waving. Toddler tucked in with the bear, waving back, eyes heavy. Every toddler book should end somewhere a child can be put to bed.

Back Matter

Keep it to two pages at this age. Parents of two-year-olds don't read six pages of back matter — but they do read a short note that tells them what to say.

Three Words to Say Together

Machine
Something people built. It doesn't eat, sleep, or grow. A toaster is a machine. So is Byte.
Learn
Getting better at something by doing it again and again — like Byte looking at cats, or you turning pages.
Ask
What you do when you're not sure. Find your grown-up and ask.

Play Along

  • Beep together. Let your child say every "beep, beep, beep!" You do the rest. This is the point of the refrain.
  • Be Byte. Point at things around the room and guess wrong on purpose — call the sofa a banana. Let them correct you. Being right about the world is a huge feeling at three.
  • Machine or not? Walk around the house asking "machine, or alive?" The fridge. The cat. The vacuum. Grandma. Trickier than it sounds, and it's the real lesson of the book.

A Note for Grown-Ups

A two-year-old cannot learn what artificial intelligence is, and this book doesn't try. It plants one idea that will still be true when they're fifteen: the machine is not alive, and it gets things wrong. Children who grow up with talking speakers in the kitchen often assume otherwise, and the assumption is surprisingly sticky. Naming it early costs nothing and helps a lot.

When they're ready for algorithms, training data, and fact-checking, the 5–8 edition — Hello! I'm A.I. — has the same robot in it.
