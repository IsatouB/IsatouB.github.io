Big Ideas for Little Minds

AI Art Prompt Pack

Character bibles, a locked style string, and a ready-to-paste prompt for every spread in all four books.

What this is, and what it isn't

I can't generate images in this session — there's no image tool wired up here. So this isn't finished art; it's the thing that makes your art come out usable instead of a mess of 64 slightly different robots.

Everything below is copy-paste ready. Take it to Midjourney, ChatGPT, Firefly, or whatever you prefer, and work through it in the order given. Do the character sheets first. If you skip straight to spread prompts because it's more fun, you will generate a hundred images and throw them all away — I promise you that.

1. Picking a tool

ToolStrengthWeakness
MidjourneyBest-looking children's illustration by a wide margin. Has real character-reference and style-reference features.Subscription; syntax changes between versions; a learning curve.
ChatGPT image generationEasiest to steer — you describe changes in plain English and it holds context across a conversation.Slower per image; style drifts over long sessions.
Adobe FireflyTrained on licensed content and built for commercial use, which is the cleanest legal footing of the bunch.Illustration style is blander than Midjourney's.
IdeogramThe only one that renders readable text in images.You shouldn't put text in images anyway — see §4.

My recommendation: Midjourney for the illustrations, because character reference is the entire ballgame on a 64-spread project. Whatever you choose, read that tool's current commercial-use terms yourself before you publish — they change often, and the terms usually differ between free and paid tiers.

2. The only real problem: consistency

Byte on page 9 must be recognisably the same robot as Byte on page 51 — same proportions, same eye, same wheels, same blue. Readers of any age notice instantly when he isn't, and it makes a book look cheap. Three things fix this, and you need all three:

  1. A character bible — a fixed block of words describing each character, pasted verbatim into every single prompt. Never paraphrase it. Never "improve" it mid-project.
  2. A locked style string — a fixed block describing the art style, also pasted into every prompt, unchanged.
  3. Reference images — generate one good character sheet per character, save it, and feed it back as a reference on every subsequent generation (--cref in Midjourney, or just uploading it in ChatGPT/Firefly).

The formula for every prompt in this document

[SCENE] + [CHARACTER BLOCK for whoever is in it] + [STYLE BLOCK] + [PARAMETERS]

The per-spread prompts in §6 and §7 give you the SCENE only. You append the other three from §3, §4, and §5 every time. That's the discipline the whole pack depends on.

3. The style block

Paste this into every prompt, unchanged, for the whole project. This is what makes 64 images look like one book.

modern children's picture book illustration, flat vector shapes with soft
paper-grain texture, bold clean outlines, rounded friendly forms, simple
uncluttered composition with generous negative space, soft even lighting,
cheerful and warm, hand-lettered picture book sensibility, no photorealism,
no 3D render, no text, no words, no letters

Then add the palette line for whichever book you're working on:

AI books — Hello! I'm A.I. and Beep! I'm Byte

color palette: deep navy blue, bright teal, sunny yellow, warm coral, cream white

Privacy books — That's Private! and Ask Me First!

color palette: deep violet purple, teal green, warm coral, soft gold, cream white

Two palettes off a shared structure is what makes the books read as a series but not as duplicates. Don't add a third.

4. Non-negotiable rules

  • Never let AI generate the text. Not the title, not the verse, not a sign in the background. AI-rendered letters are subtly wrong in a way that screams amateur, and you can't fix kerning in a JPEG. Generate clean art with empty space where words go, then set every word in Canva, Affinity Publisher, or InDesign. This is why no text, no words, no letters is in the style block.
  • Leave room for the words. Add large empty area in the upper third for text placement to any spread whose verse sits over the art.
  • Aspect ratios. Full-bleed spread → --ar 2:1. Single page → --ar 1:1 for the 8"×8" toddler books, --ar 1:1 for the 8.5"×8.5" older books.
  • Resolution. KDP needs 300 DPI. An 8"×8" page with bleed is 2,475 px square; a full spread is about 4,875 × 2,475 px. No generator outputs that natively — generate at maximum, then upscale 2–4× with a dedicated upscaler before layout.
  • Nothing important in the gutter. On a two-page spread, keep faces and key objects out of the middle 1.5 inches or they'll disappear into the spine.

The toddler books need their own child protagonist — a 3-year-old will not identify with a 7-year-old. Nia is Maya and Amir's little cousin, which lets all four books share a world without needing to share a cast.

Grown-ups

Character block a warm, ordinary parent in their thirties, relaxed everyday clothes, kind unhurried expression, crouching to child height whenever speaking to a child

Vary the grown-ups' appearance across spreads — different skin tones, ages, and genders. Children should see that a trusted grown-up doesn't have one look.

Before you go further

Generate all four character sheets now. Look at them side by side. Are you happy? Because you are about to draw these characters sixty-four more times, and changing your mind at spread 40 means regenerating everything before it. This is the cheapest hour you will spend on the project.

8. Prompts for the 5–8 books

I haven't written out all 54 of those here, because you already have them. The art notes inside Hello! I'm A.I. and That's Private! were written as prompts. Convert each one like this:

1. Copy the art note text from the manuscript spread.
2. Delete the production asides — "series continuity matters",
   "design this to work as a classroom poster", character ages
   in parentheses, and any sentence addressed to the illustrator.
3. Replace character names with the character blocks from §5.
4. Append the style block + the correct palette + --ar 2:1.

Worked example, from Book 1, spread 16:

Raw art note The classic gag done big: a tray of blueberry muffins next to a row of chihuahua faces, genuinely hard to tell apart. Byte pointing confidently at a muffin with a label reading "PUPPY — 98% sure." Kids laughing.
Converted prompt a tray of blueberry muffins beside a row of chihuahua faces arranged in a grid, comically similar to each other, a small friendly robot pointing confidently at one muffin with an antenna-arm, two children laughing in the background, empty banner space above the muffin for a label to be added later + [BYTE BLOCK] + [MAYA BLOCK] + [AMIR BLOCK] + [STYLE BLOCK] + [AI PALETTE] --ar 2:1

Note the label became empty banner space. That's rule one from §4, applied — never let the generator write "PUPPY — 98% sure," because it will come out as "PUPPPY — 9B% sme."

10. From generated image to printable page

  1. Generate at maximum resolution, then upscale 2–4× with a dedicated upscaler. Check the result at 100% zoom — upscalers invent nonsense in faces and hands, and faces are most of your art.
  2. Fix the small stuff. Six-fingered hands, a wheel that grew, an eye that drifted. Every spread will need something. Budget for it.
  3. Lay out in Canva, Affinity Publisher, or InDesign at 8.25" × 8.25" per page with bleed (8.75" × 8.75" for the 8.5" books). Place art, then set all type over it.
  4. Use one typeface for the whole series. A chunky rounded sans for the verse. Buy a commercial license for it — free-for-personal-use fonts are a real trap when you're selling.
  5. Keep type off busy areas. If a line isn't readable, put a soft translucent shape behind it — never squeeze the type smaller.
  6. Export PDF/X, 300 DPI, fonts embedded, transparency flattened. Run it through KDP's previewer and look at every page.
  7. Order a proof copy. Screen colors are lit from behind; paper isn't. Navy in particular prints darker than you expect.

11. Legal, honestly

  • Disclose it to KDP. Amazon asks whether your content is AI-generated. Images count. Answer yes. It isn't shown to customers and it doesn't hurt your listing — but a false answer puts your entire account at risk, which is not a trade worth making for a checkbox.
  • You likely don't own the AI images. Under current US Copyright Office guidance, purely AI-generated images aren't protected by copyright. You can sell the book — people do, every day — but you may have no recourse if someone lifts your art. Your text is fully yours either way, and so is the book as a compiled whole.
  • Check your tool's terms. Commercial rights differ by service and by tier, and they get revised. Read them at the moment you publish, not from memory.
  • Still no Seuss. Don't prompt "in the style of Dr. Seuss," don't put a red-and-white striped hat on anything, and don't name any living illustrator in a prompt.

12. Work order

  • Pick your tool and read its commercial terms.
  • Generate character sheets for Byte and Nia. Save as reference images.
  • Generate the Beep! I'm Byte cover ten times. Pick one. This tells you if the style works.
  • Generate all 16 spreads of Beep! I'm Byte, in order, same reference image every time.
  • Upscale, retouch, lay out, export, proof.
  • Publish it. Then look at what sold before making the other three.

Start with the toddler book

Sixteen spreads instead of twenty-seven, 32 pages instead of 48, simpler art, lower print cost, and a bigger buying audience — ages 2–5 is the highest-volume segment in children's books, because that's where gifts and repeat reads live. Beep! I'm Byte is by a wide margin the cheapest way to find out whether this series works, and everything you learn doing it makes the other three faster.
