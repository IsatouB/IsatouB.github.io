Big Ideas for Little Minds

Publishing Guide

Everything between a finished manuscript and a live Amazon listing — for Hello! I'm A.I. and That's Private!

Read this first: the legal line

The book you showed me is from The Cat in the Hat's Learning Library, which is owned by Dr. Seuss Enterprises. What I wrote for you copies the format — rhyming nonfiction, a guide character, ages 5–8, back matter — because formats aren't ownable. The characters, verse, titles, and series name are entirely original.

Three things you must never do when you publish these:

  • Don't use the words Cat in the Hat, Learning Library, or Seuss anywhere — not in the title, subtitle, description, or keyword fields. Using a trademark as a keyword is the fastest way to get a listing pulled.
  • Don't let your illustrator draw in recognizable Seuss trade dress — the red-and-white striped hat, that specific character, that exact lettering.
  • Don't write "in the style of Dr. Seuss" in your description. "Perfect for fans of read-aloud rhyming science books" says the same thing and is safe.

Byte, Maya, and Amir are yours. Keep them consistent and they become the asset — a series mascot is worth more than any single book.

2. Getting it illustrated

Option A — hire a human illustrator

Expect roughly $100–$400 per spread for a professional children's illustrator, so $3,000–$10,000 for a 28-spread book. Cheaper on Fiverr/Upwork ($40–$120 a spread), more on Reedsy or an agency. Always: get a written contract, buy full commercial rights (not a license), and pay in milestones — character sheets, then rough sketches for all spreads, then finals. Never pay in full up front.

Give them the manuscript with art notes visible, and ask first for a character sheet of Byte, Maya, and Amir from three angles. Approve that before a single spread is drawn. Consistency across 56 pages is the hardest part of illustrating a picture book.

Option B — generate the art with AI

Far cheaper and much faster, with three real catches you should know before you commit:

  • You must disclose it to KDP. Amazon asks during publishing whether the content is AI-generated. Images you generated count. Answer honestly — the disclosure isn't shown to customers, but a false answer risks your whole account.
  • Purely AI-generated images generally aren't copyrightable in the US. You can still sell the book, but you may not be able to stop someone from reusing the art.
  • Character consistency is genuinely hard. Byte on page 9 must be the same robot on page 51. Budget real time for this, and lock a reference image of each character first.

Option C — hybrid

Generate rough art to test the book with actual children, then commission a human for the final. This is the smart order of operations: find out whether kids sit still for it before you spend four thousand dollars.

For a 48-page color book the spine lands around a tenth of an inch, which is too thin for spine text; plan for a spine that is solid color only.

4. Cover briefs

Book 1 — Hello! I'm A.I.

Deep blue-to-teal background, the way the reference book uses deep navy. Byte in the center, big and friendly and glowing, with Maya and Amir floating around him inside soft circles of light made of code and pattern shapes. Title in chunky rounded sans-serif, white, top-left, stacked over three lines. Subtitle in warm yellow beneath it. Small series badge in the top corner reading "BIG IDEAS FOR LITTLE MINDS" — same badge position on both books.

Book 2 — That's Private!

Same layout, same badge, same lettering — swap the palette to deep purple and teal so they're obviously a pair. Maya and Amir front and center holding a glowing treasure chest, with Byte beside them. Warm and confident, never anxious. A parent scanning the shelf should read "safety, handled calmly," not "danger."

The thumbnail test. Shrink your cover to the size of a postage stamp. If the title isn't readable and the character isn't recognizable at that size, redo it. That stamp is the only version most Amazon shoppers will ever see.

6. Pricing and royalties

Full-color printing is expensive, and this is where most first-time picture-book publishers get a nasty surprise. Paperback pays a flat 60% royalty minus the printing cost — there's no 70% tier on paperback.

FormatSuggested priceRough print costRough royalty
Paperback, premium color, 48pp$12.99~$5.80~$2.00
Paperback, standard color, 48pp$9.99~$2.30~$3.70
Kindle (fixed-layout)$4.99—~$3.40 at 70%

Those print costs are ballpark figures and Amazon changes them — run your real page count through the KDP printing cost calculator before you set a price. The pattern holds either way: price a full-color picture book below about $9.99 and you will make almost nothing per copy.

For the Kindle version, a picture book needs fixed-layout, not reflowable — use Kindle Create. Reflowable text will scramble your spreads.

7. ISBNs

KDP gives you a free ISBN for each paperback. It works fine, but it lists Amazon as the publisher and can only be used for KDP printing. If you ever want bookstores, libraries, or another printer, buy your own from Bowker (US) — about $125 for one, or roughly $295 for ten, which is the only sensible purchase if you're planning a series. Kindle editions don't need an ISBN at all; they get an ASIN automatically.

8. Launch checklist

  • Read both manuscripts aloud to a real 6-year-old. Watch where they fidget. Cut those spreads.
  • Lock character sheets for Byte, Maya, and Amir before any spread art begins.
  • Illustrate Book 1 completely; don't start Book 2 until Book 1 is on sale.
  • Lay out the interior at 8.625" × 8.75" with bleed; export PDF/X at 300 DPI.
  • Download KDP's cover template for your exact final page count.
  • Order a physical proof copy. Do not skip this — colors shift in print.
  • Answer the AI-content disclosure question honestly.
  • Set age range 5–8 and grade range K–3.
  • Publish Book 1, then add Book 2 to the same Amazon series page so they cross-sell.
  • Ask for reviews from real readers — teachers and librarians especially. Never buy reviews.

The one piece of strategy that matters

Series sell children's books; single titles mostly don't. Two books with the same mascot, the same badge, the same trim, and the same lettering are worth far more than two unrelated books, because a parent who liked the first one buys the second without thinking. Everything in these manuscripts — Byte reappearing, the deliberate bridge from Book 1's spread 20 into Book 2, the matching sunset endings — is built to make that happen. Protect the consistency.
