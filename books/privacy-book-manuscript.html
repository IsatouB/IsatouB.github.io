Big Ideas for Little Minds
Book Two

That's Private!

All About Privacy, Passwords & Staying Safe Online

A rhyming picture book for curious kids

Ages 5–8  ·  Reading level: Grades 1–3
28 spreads  ·  48 pages  ·  approx. 1,500 words
Trim: 8.5" × 8.5" square, full color

How to Read This Manuscript

Same format as Book One. Big type is printed text; the purple box is the art note for your illustrator. Red-edged art notes mark the emotionally sensitive spreads — those need warm color and calm faces, never fear. A frightened child stops listening.

The Story

Spread 1 — pages 4–5
Byte rolled back in Saturday, right after lunch, with a beep and a blink and a wave and a hunch. "Today," said the robot, "we're learning a word. It's a word about YOU — and the best one you've heard."
Art — Sunny kitchen, crumbs and juice glasses still on the table. BYTE (identical design to Book One) rolling in through the doorway, antenna raised. MAYA and AMIR spin around mid-bite, thrilled to see him again. Series continuity matters — same room, same house, same kids.
Spread 2 — pages 6–7
He spun in a circle. He flashed his small light. "The word," said Byte, "is PRIVACY. Right? It means some things are yours. They belong just to you. And YOU get to choose who can see them. It's true!"
Art — The word PRIVACY hand-lettered enormous across the whole spread, with the kids climbing on the letters. Inside the letter O, a little scene of Maya holding a treasure box close to her chest.
Spread 3 — pages 8–9
"You shut the bathroom door. Now that is privacy. You whisper to a friend. And that is privacy. Your diary. Your daydream. The wish on your cake. Not all things are for all folks — and that's not a mistake."
Art — Four cheerful vignettes: a closed bathroom door with a "knock please" sign; two kids whispering behind cupped hands; a locked diary; a birthday cake mid-wish with eyes squeezed shut. Everyday, ordinary, safe. Privacy starts as a normal thing, not a scary thing.
Spread 4 — pages 10–11
"Now PRIVATE is not the same thing as BAD. Private just means: it is mine, and I'm glad. There's nothing gone wrong when you keep something small… but there's one kind of secret you must tell. Tell it all."
Art — Left page bright and light: a child happily holding a small closed box labeled MINE. Right page: the same box, but heavy, gray, and too big to carry — with the child looking uncertain. Sets up spread 5 visually before a single word of it is read.
Spread 5 — pages 12–13
"A SURPRISE is a party, a gift, or a cake. It is happy. It ends. And it's fun when it's shared. But a secret that's heavy, that hurts, or feels bad? You should never keep that one. Go tell. Don't be scared."
Art — sensitive — Left: a joyful surprise party bursting with color. Right: a child walking toward a warm, kneeling parent, shoulders visibly lighter as they speak. Show the relief, not the fear. No shadowy figures, no threat imagery anywhere in this book.
Spread 6 — pages 14–15
"So who is your grown-up? Let's name a few now: a parent. A teacher. An auntie. A coach. Pick THREE that you trust. Say them right out loud. Those three are your team. And your team's who you approach."
Art — Three portrait frames with warm faces, and empty frames beside them the child can imagine filling. Byte holding up a clipboard headed "MY TEAM: 1. 2. 3." Deliberately mirrors the fill-in page in the back matter.
Spread 7 — pages 16–17
"Now here's the important bit," Byte said with a spin. "Some facts about you should stay safely tucked in. Your full name. Your address. Your school and your street. Your birthday. Your phone. And your passwords — complete!"
Art — A treasure chest with each private fact drawn as a labeled gem being tucked inside: NAME, ADDRESS, SCHOOL, BIRTHDAY, PHONE, PASSWORD. Design this list so it reads as a checklist a child can memorize — it's the spine of the whole book.
Spread 8 — pages 18–19
"But why?" asked Amir. "They're just facts about me!" "They are," answered Byte. "But now listen. You'll see. Put a few of them together and somebody could guess where you'll be at half three. And that is a mess."
Art — Three innocent puzzle pieces — a school logo, a street name, a soccer jersey — sliding together into one picture of a child's daily route. Illustrate the idea of piecing together, with no menacing character present. The concept is the lesson, not a villain.
Spread 9 — pages 20–21
"A PASSWORD," said Byte, "is a key made of letters. A short one is weak. And a long one is better. Not '1-2-3-4.' Not your birthday. Not 'cat.' Make it LONG. Make it SILLY. Yes — sillier than that!"
PurpleWaffleMoonTruck47!
Art — A row of keys: a tiny bent key labeled "1234" snapping in a lock, next to an enormous ornate key made of purple waffles, a moon, and a truck, sliding smoothly into a huge vault door. The silly password should be genuinely funny and genuinely memorable.
Spread 10 — pages 22–23
"Use a different one everywhere. Don't use one for them all — for if one gets found out, then they all take a fall. And who do you tell? Just your grown-up. That's it. Not your friend. Not a stranger. Not a website. Not me."
Art — Row of doors, all opening with one sad little key (dominoes falling behind them). Beneath it, the fixed version: each door with its own wild key. Byte holding an antenna up in a "don't tell even me" gesture — a nice character beat for the mascot.
Spread 11 — pages 24–25
"Now, apps are like guests who come knocking around. They say, 'May I see you? May I hear your sound? May I know where you are? May I look at your face?' Before you say yes — ask a grown-up. Just in case."
Art — Cartoon app-characters at a front door holding permission cards: a camera, a microphone, a map pin, a contacts book. Maya at the door with a parent's hand on the latch. Polite, not sinister — apps ask, and you get to answer.
Spread 12 — pages 26–27
"And here's a strange thing about screens, big and small: they remember. They keep things. They don't forget at all. Every message and picture and clip that you send leaves a FOOTPRINT behind you. And footprints don't end."
Art — A trail of glowing footprints leading out of a phone and off across a wide landscape — through a park, past a school, over a hill, disappearing to the horizon. Beautiful rather than scary. This is the image people will remember from the book.
Spread 13 — pages 28–29
"So before you hit SEND, take a breath. Count to three. Ask: 'Would I be all right if my whole school could see?' If the answer is no, then don't send it. Delete. Once a message is out, you can never retreat."
Art — Amir's thumb hovering over a big SEND button, with a thought-bubble showing his entire class, his teacher, and his grandmother all reading the message. He pauses. Give the pause its own visual weight — a held breath.
Spread 14 — pages 30–31
"And why is that game free?" Byte asked with a wink. "It isn't quite free. Let me tell you. Just think: they are learning what you like — your clicks and your play — and they use it to sell you more things every day."
Art — A bright arcade-style game with a big FREE! banner. Behind the machine, a conveyor belt carrying little tagged data-parcels — LIKES CATS, PLAYS AT 7PM, WANTS SNEAKERS — off toward a shop full of advertisements. Reveal-the-machinery composition.
Spread 15 — pages 32–33
"Online, you can't see who is there on the other end. A grown-up can pretend to be a kid. Or a friend. A picture can be fake. And a name can be, too. So a stranger online is a STRANGER to you."
Art — sensitive — A chat window with a cheerful cartoon-puppy profile picture; beside it, a plain gray question-mark silhouette holding that same puppy picture like a mask. Neutral gray shape only — no leering adult, no menace. The point is unknowable, not evil.
Spread 16 — pages 34–35
"If a stranger asks, 'Hi! What's your name? Where's your school?' — don't answer. Go tell. That is always the rule. If they ask for a photo, the answer is NO. If they say, 'Don't tell anyone' — TELL. And then GO."
Art — Three bold instruction panels like road signs: DON'T ANSWER / SAY NO / GO TELL. Maya already up and walking toward a grown-up, chin high and confident. She is the hero of this page, not a victim.
Spread 17 — pages 36–37
"There's a feeling you get. It's a tug. It's a knot. It's a hot little wobble right there in your gut. That's your body just saying, 'Hey — wait! Something's weird!' So listen. Close the screen. Go and tell. Never fear."
Art — sensitive — Amir at a tablet, a soft orange glow drawn in his tummy like a little warning lantern. Next panel: the tablet face-down, and Amir walking to find Dad. Naming the physical feeling is the most useful thing in this book — give it a full page.
Spread 18 — pages 38–39
"If a box on your screen shouts YOU WON! CLICK RIGHT HERE! — don't click it. It's a trick. Let me make that quite clear. Prizes don't fall from a screen like the rain. Close the window. Don't touch it. Then go and explain."
Art — A gaudy blinking pop-up with confetti and a cartoon trophy — drawn to look exactly like the real thing, because recognition is the skill. Byte pulling Maya's hand gently back from the screen. Behind the pop-up, a paper-thin fake with nothing behind it.
Spread 19 — pages 40–41
"Now privacy works in two ways. Here's the deal: other people have privacy, too. It's for real. So ask first, before posting a picture of Sam. And if Sam says 'No thank you' — then you don't. Yes, ma'am!"
Art — Maya holding up a phone to photograph a friend, who has a hand raised politely: "not right now." Maya lowering the phone with a smile — no sulking. Model the graceful acceptance of a "no"; that's the half of consent nobody teaches.
Spread 20 — pages 42–43
"Don't pass on a secret. Don't share what they said. Don't post where they live. Don't repeat what you read. Be the friend who can hold something safe in her hand. That's a very good friend. That's a very good plan."
Art — Two kids on a step, one cupping something glowing and small in both hands and keeping it safe. Warm evening light. A quiet, tender spread — the emotional counterweight to all the rules.
Spread 21 — pages 44–45
"Some apps draw a map with a dot. And it's YOU. It can show where you sleep and the things that you do. Ask a grown-up to check it. Ask, 'Should this be on?' And most of the time? The right answer is: gone."
Art — A map of a neighborhood with a bright dot and a dotted line tracing the daily route: home → school → park → home. On the facing page, a hand tapping a toggle to OFF and the trail fading away. Show the fix, always show the fix.
Spread 22 — pages 46–47
"And the most private thing? It's your body. It's you. The parts a swimsuit covers belong just to you. No one should ask for a picture. No one at all. And if somebody does — that's a TELL. Make that call."
Art — sensitive — Kids at a sunny pool in swimsuits, laughing, entirely ordinary. Byte beside them under a small banner: MY BODY BELONGS TO ME. Nothing on this page should look clinical, secretive, or shadowed. Bright daylight only.
Spread 23 — pages 48–49
"You can say, 'No thank you.' You can say, 'I don't want to.' You can hand back the tablet and say, 'I am through.' And you'll never be in trouble for coming to tell — not with your grown-ups. Say it once. All is well."
Art — Amir handing a tablet to a parent, relieved and unbothered. Parent's face open and unsurprised — no scolding, no phone-confiscation drama. Children stay quiet because they fear the reaction; this page exists to defuse that.
Spread 24 — pages 50–51
"So make rules with your family. Write them. Hang them high: where you use screens, and for how long, and why. Rules aren't a punishment. Rules are a fence. They keep all the fun in — and keep out the nonsense."
Art — The whole family at the fridge, taping up a hand-drawn rules chart with crayon and mismatched handwriting — clearly made together. A fenced yard visible through the window, kids playing safely inside it. Mirrors the fill-in chart in the back matter.
Spread 25 — pages 52–53
"Let's remember together. Now say it with me: My name and my address stay in. (Take a bow!) My passwords are long, and I don't pass them out. And when something feels funny — I TELL. And right now."
Art — Kids and Byte standing shoulder to shoulder mid-chant, mouths open, fists up. Big, bold, poster-like lettering of the three promises. This is the second poster spread in the series — design it to be photographed and pinned to a classroom wall.
Spread 26 — pages 54–55
"Because YOU are the boss of the you that is you: of your face, of your name, of the things that you do. And a boss gets to choose. And a boss gets to say, 'That is private. That's mine. And not today.'"
Art — Maya and Amir drawn large and golden, standing tall like small monarchs with paper crowns, one hand each raised in a friendly STOP. The most empowering image in the book. Big color, big confidence.
Spread 27 — pages 56–57
Then Byte gave a beep and he rolled down the hall. "Keep it private," he called. "You're the boss. That is all." And we waved from the window and watched the sun set — the most sensible, safest two kids Byte had met.
Art — Sunset through the front window, long orange light across the floor. Byte's small silhouette rolling down the path, one antenna waving. Kids at the glass. Echo the closing composition of Book One so the two books rhyme visually on a shelf.

Back Matter

Six pages. The fill-in pages are the reason a parent buys the paperback instead of borrowing the library copy — they get written in.

Words to Know

Privacy
Getting to choose who sees, hears, or knows something about you.
Private information
Facts that should stay with you and your grown-ups: full name, address, school, phone number, birthday, and passwords.
Password
A key made of letters and numbers. Long and silly is strong. Short and easy is weak.
Trusted adult
A grown-up you know in real life who keeps you safe and never makes you keep a heavy secret.
Permission
Asking first — and accepting the answer, even when it's no.
Digital footprint
The trail of messages, photos, and clicks you leave behind you online.
Stranger
Someone you don't know in real life. Online, everyone you haven't met in person is a stranger.
Pop-up
A box that jumps onto your screen. Many are tricks. Close them; don't click them.
Location
Where you are right now. Apps can see it — unless a grown-up turns it off.
The uh-oh feeling
The tug in your tummy that says something's wrong. Always listen to it.

Build a Super Password

Pick one word from each row. Stick them together. Add a number that isn't your birthday, and one squiggle mark.

  • A color: purple, gold, lime, navy
  • A snack: waffle, mango, pretzel, noodle
  • A big thing: moon, tractor, volcano, whale
  • A number + squiggle: 47!   92?   15#

My super password lives in my head and in my grown-up's password keeper — nowhere else.

Say It Out Loud

Practice these with a grown-up until they come out easily. Having the words ready is what makes a child able to use them under pressure.

"I have to ask my grown-up first."
"No thank you. I don't share that."
"I'm going to go tell someone."
"Please don't post that picture of me."

Talk About It

  1. Name three things that are private about you.
  2. What's the difference between a surprise and a secret that feels heavy?
  3. Why is a long silly password stronger than a short easy one?
  4. Someone online says, "Don't tell your mom." What do you do?
  5. Your friend says no to a photo. How do you answer?

A Note for Grown-Ups

Children are handed screens years before they're handed the vocabulary to describe what's happening on them. This book gives them that vocabulary early, while the stakes are still low.

Three things worth reinforcing after you close the book. First, the uh-oh feeling on spread 17 — a child who can name that sensation is far more likely to act on it. Second, the promise on spread 23 that they will never be in trouble for telling; children most often stay silent because they fear losing the device or disappointing you, so say it out loud and mean it. Third, that privacy runs in both directions — spreads 19 and 20 ask them to protect other people's information too, which is the part that makes them a good friend rather than just a careful one.

Read it more than once. Rules learned in rhyme stick around a lot longer than rules learned in a lecture.
