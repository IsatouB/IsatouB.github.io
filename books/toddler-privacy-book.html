Ask Me First!

A First Book About Privacy
Ages 2–5  ·  Read-aloud / pre-reader
16 spreads  ·  32 pages  ·  196 words
Trim: 8" × 8" square, full color

What changed for ages 2–5

Passwords, digital footprints, and online strangers are all gone — they mean nothing to a three-year-old. What's left is the part that actually matters at this age, and it's the foundation everything else gets built on later:

  • Ownership first. Some things are mine. Toddlers already understand "mine" intensely — this book borrows that feeling and gives it a name.
  • Consent in both directions. Ask me first, and I ask you first. Teaching only the first half produces a child who guards themselves and grabs from others.
  • Body autonomy, calmly. The swimsuit rule, the right to refuse a hug, and the doctor exception — stated plainly, in daylight, with no fear anywhere on the page.
  • One screen rule: ask a grown-up first. That's all a preschooler needs.
  • Telling is the hero move. Two spreads make it the brave, correct, never-in-trouble thing.

Format note — there are no board books on KDP

KDP prints paperback and hardcover only. Publish this as an 8" × 8" full-color paperback and call it a "first book" or "read-aloud picture book," never a board book — buyers who expect thick pages and get thin ones leave one-star reviews.

The Story

Spread 1 — pages 2–3
Beep, beep, beep! Look who came! Byte is back. Let's play a game.
Art — BYTE rolling in, identical design to Beep! I'm Byte. Same doorway, same house. A toddler on the rug looking up, delighted. The shared "beep, beep, beep!" refrain opens both toddler books — that's what makes them feel like a set.
Spread 2 — pages 4–5
Some things are for EVERYONE: the park, the slide, the great big sun!
Art — A wide, sunny playground full of many different children sharing a slide, a sandbox, a swing. Warm and generous and busy. Establishing "shared" first makes "private" land properly on the next page.
Spread 3 — pages 6–7
But some things are mine. Just mine. It's true: my bear, my cup, my toothbrush too!
Art — Three objects, big and clear on a plain background: a well-loved teddy bear, a favorite cup, a toothbrush. The toddler holding all three at once, arms full. Objects a small child will recognize as theirs immediately.
Spread 4 — pages 8–9
There's a word for that. PRIVACY! It means some things belong to me.
Art — The word PRIVACY hand-lettered enormous and playful across the whole spread, the toddler and Byte peeking through the letters. Even pre-readers learn to recognize a word shape when it's this big and this repeated.
Spread 5 — pages 10–11
Knock, knock, knock! Please wait for me. The door is closed. That's privacy!
Art — A closed bathroom door with a crayon sign taped to it. A hand knocking. Byte waiting patiently outside with his antenna politely lowered. Sound-word page — kids will knock on the book.
Spread 6 — pages 12–13
My tummy's mine. My toes are mine. My ears! My nose! All mine. All mine!
Art — Toddler pointing at each body part in turn across four little panels, giggling. Bright, silly, no clothing removed — everyday clothes throughout. A pointing-and-naming page, which is the most-reread kind at this age.
Spread 7 — pages 14–15
The parts my swimsuit covers up are private, private, PRIVATE. Yup!
Art — sensitive — Bright poolside or sprinkler scene, children in ordinary swimsuits, laughing, full daylight. Nothing clinical, nothing shadowed, no anatomy, no arrows or diagrams. The tone is a sunny afternoon, and the rule is just stated out loud in the middle of it.
Spread 8 — pages 16–17
If a doctor checks to keep me well, my grown-up stays. And I can tell.
Art — sensitive — A friendly doctor with a stethoscope, the toddler on the exam table, and a parent sitting right beside them with a hand on the child's back. The parent must be clearly visible in frame — that presence is the entire point of the spread.
Spread 9 — pages 18–19
"No, thank you!" I can say. A wave, a high-five — that's okay!
Art — A relative leaning in for a hug; the toddler holding up a hand and offering a high-five instead, cheerful and unbothered. The grown-up smiling and accepting it easily. Model the adult taking "no" gracefully — that's the half that changes households.
Spread 10 — pages 20–21
Ask me first before a squeeze. Ask me first for pictures, please!
Art — Two panels: someone crouching to ask before a hug, and someone holding up a phone camera and asking before a photo. Toddler nodding yes in one, shaking head no in the other. Both answers shown as equally fine.
Spread 11 — pages 22–23
And I ask YOU before I take your bear, your book, your slice of cake.
Art — The toddler asking another child before picking up their teddy. Hands open, not grabbing. The second child considering. This spread is the reason the book is called Ask Me First! rather than That's Mine!
Spread 12 — pages 24–25
If you say no, I say, "Okay!" Your stuff is yours. Let's go and play!
Art — The friend shaking their head; our toddler shrugging happily and running off toward blocks with them anyway. No sulk, no tantrum, friendship intact. Toddlers need to see that a "no" doesn't end the fun.
Spread 13 — pages 26–27
Beep! A screen! Can I? Can I? Ask a grown-up first. Then try!
Art — Toddler reaching up toward a tablet on a table, then turning to a parent with a questioning face. Parent nodding and handing it down together. Byte beside the tablet with a small "ask first" gesture.
Spread 14 — pages 28–29
A brand-new face inside my screen? I go tell my grown-up what I've seen.
Art — sensitive — A tablet showing a plain, neutral gray question-mark avatar in a video-call window. The toddler already climbing down and walking toward a parent. No adult figure, no threat, no shadow — an unknown is a question mark, nothing more.
Spread 15 — pages 30–31
If someone says, "Don't tell. Don't tell." I TELL right now. And all is well.
Art — sensitive — Just the toddler, running with purpose across a bright room into a parent's open arms, both smiling. Do not illustrate the person who said "don't tell" at all — leave that frame empty. The image is the telling and the welcome, full stop.
Spread 16 — pages 32–33
I'm the boss of ME, ME, ME! My body. My things. My PRIVACY!
Art — The toddler standing tall in the middle of the spread in a paper crown, arms up, bear under one arm, absolutely triumphant. Byte cheering at their feet, antenna waving goodbye. Biggest, brightest, most confident page in the book — end on the child, always.

Back Matter

Three Words to Say Together

Privacy
Some things are mine. I get to choose who sees them.
Ask
What we say before we hug, take, or share — every single time.
Tell
What I do when something feels funny. I'm never in trouble for telling.

Say It Out Loud

Practice these until they come out easily. A child who has said the words before can say them when it counts.

"No, thank you."
"Ask me first!"
"Can I have a turn, please?"
"I'm going to tell."

Play Along

  • Mine or everyone's? Point around the room. The couch? Everyone's. Your toothbrush? Yours. The park? Everyone's. Your bear? Yours. Silly ones are the best ones.
  • Knock, knock. Let them knock on the page. Then practice knocking on a real closed door and waiting for "come in."
  • Ask first, at home. For one week, ask your child before you pick them up, hug them, or photograph them. It feels strange for about two days. Then it feels normal — and they will start asking you back.

A Note for Grown-Ups

Two things in this book do most of the work, and both are for you rather than your child.

The first is spread 9. When a child says no to a hug and the adult accepts it warmly, the child learns that their "no" has power. When the adult overrules it — even kindly, even for Grandma — they learn the opposite, and that lesson transfers. This is the single highest-leverage page in the book, and it only works if you back it up in real life.

The second is spread 15. Children stay silent because they expect trouble. Say "you will never be in trouble for telling me" out loud, more than once, and then honour it the first time they test it — including when what they tell you is inconvenient.

When they're older, That's Private! (ages 5–8) picks this up with passwords, screens, and online strangers.
